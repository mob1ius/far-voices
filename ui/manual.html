Section 1.0

What you are looking at

A spacecraft cockpit in a browser that sings you the actual radio traffic between Earth and everything humans have left out there.

Here is the real machinery behind it. NASA runs enormous dish antennas at three sites spread evenly around the planet, in California, Spain and Australia, so that no matter which way Earth is turned, at least one of them is facing deep space. Together they are called the Deep Space Network, and they are the only way anyone talks to Voyager, to the Mars orbiters, to the spacecraft currently falling toward an asteroid. NASA publishes what those dishes are doing right now, updated every few seconds, for anyone to read.

This site reads that feed and turns it into a choir. Each spacecraft in contact becomes a voice. A stronger signal sings louder; a faster data rate picks a higher voice. When a dish locks onto a spacecraft, the music changes to mark the moment. Nothing is invented and nothing is pre-recorded. If the choir suddenly gains a bass voice, a real antenna somewhere just started hearing a real machine that is very far away.

Table 1-1 · How a link becomes a voice
What the dish is receivingWhat you hear
Under 40 kb/sBass
40 to 250 kb/sTenor
250 to 800 kb/sAlto
Over 800 kb/sSoprano
A 70-metre dishThe octavist, an octave below the bass
Anything at all on the airThe ison, the drone that holds underneath

Received power sets how loudly a voice sings, so a faint craft is a whisper. A spacecraft running two downlinks at once can land in two voices at the same time, each scored by its own data rate, neither aware the other exists.

The cockpit around the music is the invented part. It is a 1960s spacecraft console, built out of photographs, with working clocks, gauges, a globe, a teleprinter, a tape deck and a great many switches. Most of those switches do something. Some of them do something you would not expect, which is what most of this manual is about.

Section 2.0

Starting up

The deck opens dark and silent with one button lit: POWER UP. Press it. Browsers refuse to make noise until a human clicks something, which is why a spacecraft that runs on sound has to ask permission first.

Once power is on, the choir begins and the deck fills in around you. The badge at the top left tells you whether you are hearing the sky as it is right now or a recording that plays when the live feed cannot be reached. Green means live. That distinction matters more than it looks: green means the voices you hear are signals that left a spacecraft minutes or hours ago and finished their journey while you were sitting there.

Sections 7 and 8 are folded shut on purpose. Everything in them is findable by pulling switches, and being handed the answers is a worse way to meet a spacecraft than finding them. They open with one click when you want them.

Then scroll. The deck keeps going for about a dozen consoles, and every one of them is a photograph with live instruments punched through it. Every one of them has something to touch. Whenever you touch something, a line of text scrolls across the amber advisory screen below the hand controller telling you what it was, what it is for, and occasionally what a real crew thought of it.

Caution

Nothing here can hurt your computer, cost you anything, or send you an email. The worst this manual can do is freeze your imaginary spacecraft, and even that thaws.

This manual describes the full deck, which needs a keyboard and a pointer. On a phone you get the choir, the map, the archive and the time machine; the ship systems in Sections 4 to 7, the advisory screen and the lower porthole are not fitted to that panel.

Keys

Space pauses. Left and right arrows jump between events. Square brackets step through tapes. F expands the map, M opens music, S opens signals, T opens the time machine, and ? opens this manual.

Section 4.0

Alarms

Real spacecraft do not politely mention that something is wrong. They have a system with exactly three volumes, caution, warning, and an alarm loud enough to wake a sleeping crew, and it climbs through them in order. This deck does the same, and the climb is the interesting part, because every step is a chance to fix things before the next one.

When a system is unhappy, its name lights an amber square on the caution and warning panel, on the sub-panel at the left of the guidance console. Nothing else happens yet. Leave it alone for twenty seconds, or break a second thing, and a two-tone warble joins the choir. Break a third and the klaxon starts, the whole deck pulses red, and the singing ducks so you can hear yourself panic.

Table 4-1 · The ladder
LevelWhat sets it offWhat the deck does
NominalNothing wrongChoir only. Most visitors never leave this state, which is fine.
CautionOne faultOne amber lamp lights. A soft warble every eight seconds.
WarningTwo faults, or one fault left alone for twenty secondsMaster caution lights, a louder warble every four seconds, the choir thins.
Master alarmThree faultsKlaxon every three seconds, red pulse across the deck, choir ducked hard, and the hull creaking.

There is one button that makes it stop, and it is a trap worth understanding. Pressing MASTER ALARM or MASTER CAUTION silences the noise for thirty seconds and drops the deck one level. It does not fix anything. The teleprinter says so in as many words: MASTER ALARM SILENCED - CONDITION UNCHANGED. The amber lamp stays lit, the fault stays on the list, and in thirty seconds the ship starts up again, because the problem is still there. Quieting an alarm and repairing a spacecraft are different jobs, and only one of them counts.

While any alarm is up, the amber advisory screen stops telling you jokes and starts telling you what is broken, what to do about it, and how long you have before the next rung.

Section 5.0

Cold and heat

Space is not cold the way a freezer is cold. A freezer is full of chilled air touching your skin; space is empty, so there is nothing to touch you at all. What actually happens is that a spacecraft glows its heat away as infrared light and never gets any back except from the sun. Left alone, the cabin cools. The only reason any crew stays comfortable is a heater quietly putting the warmth back.

So there is a CABIN HEAT switch at the left of the guidance console, beside the thruster pad. Turn it off and the temperature starts falling toward about 4 °C, which is unpleasant but survivable. Turn the CABIN FAN on as well, which lives on the footer next to the power switch, and it heads for about −18 °C instead, because moving air strips heat off everything it touches. That is the joke buried in the design: you will freeze your spacecraft because you wanted fresh air, and the two switches that do it are a whole console apart.

Heat has an opposite, and it is funnier. Leave the heater on with the fan off and the cabin sits comfortable. Run the microwave at the same time and it climbs about twelve degrees higher, and above 30 °C the deck starts to sweat: water beads on the glass and everything looks slightly ill. Turn the microwave off, or the fan on, and it passes.

Section 6.0

Spinning, and stopping

On Earth, if you spin a chair and let go, friction stops it. In orbit there is no friction and nothing to push against. Start a spacecraft turning and it turns forever, until you spend something to stop it, and what you spend is gas, squirted out of small nozzles called thrusters. You have a finite amount. When it is gone, it is gone.

The joystick on the guidance console works this way. In its normal mode it does not point the ship; it makes the ship turn, and the turning continues after you let go. Three numbers on the flight director console, in the band beside the abort guard, show how fast it is turning in degrees per second around each axis: pitch is nose up and down, yaw is nose left and right, roll is the ship rotating like a drill bit. The stars in both windows wheel past to match, because the sky you are looking at is the sky the ship is pointed at.

Getting it back

Above 2 degrees per second the ATT RATES lamp lights. Above 6 the ship is tumbling. Recovery is a genuine skill, and it goes like this.

Table 6-1 · Rate null procedure
StepDo thisBecause
1Read the three rate numbers and find the biggest one.Fixing the worst axis first is the difference between thirty seconds and five minutes.
2Push the stick against that rotation.The stick is the coarse control, and it burns about three percent of your propellant per second held.
3Below about half a degree per second, let go of the stick and tap the thruster buttons instead.Each tap removes exactly 0.2 degrees per second and costs almost nothing. The stick is too blunt an instrument down here.
4With every axis under 0.5, engage ATT HOLD.The ship then holds that attitude by itself. Try it above the limit and it refuses and tells you why.

Two things help you, and neither of them is a tutorial popup. A green cross appears on the attitude ball itself showing which way to push, so following the cross is never wrong. The sound tells you too: a rising chirp means that tap helped, a falling one means it did not. And if you are still tumbling after forty-five seconds, the ship gives up on you politely, prints AUTO RATE DAMP ENGAGED, and spends its own propellant bringing you back to rest.

Caution

You will run out of propellant. Everyone runs out of propellant. When you do, the thrusters answer with a thud and RCS DEPLETED, and there is exactly one way left to stop spinning: spin the tape reels. Press rewind on the transport and the heavy reels take a degree per second of turn out of the ship. You get three of those. Real spacecraft do this with proper reaction wheels. You are doing it with a tape deck because you spent all the gas showing off.

Section 7.0

The lower porthole

A second window, below the crew station, deliberately bigger than the one at the top.

A heavy machined frame, layered glass, a roller blind stowed above it, a sill with a grab rail, and a stowage net holding a camera. It looks ninety degrees off to the side of the top window, so the two feel like two places on one ship rather than two copies of one screen. Everything else in this manual shows up here: turning rates wheel the stars past it, attitude hold stops them, and a cold cabin grows frost across it from the corners.

Show how it works, and what goes past Section 7 gives away things the window is meant to show you on its own.

Section 9.0

Clearing a fault

The same page the flight checklist on the comms console gives you, in one place.

Table 9-1 · Corrective action
LampWhat to do
MAIN BUSClose the power switch on the footer. If it is already closed, stop one of the appliances drawing on it: food warmer, percolator, card reader. Running all three at once will brown the deck out.
CABIN TEMPCabin heat on at the guidance console, cabin fan off on the footer, then wait about a minute. Below freezing the glass ices over. It still recovers.
ATT RATESPush the hand controller against the rotation, watching the three rate numbers fall toward zero, then trim with the four thruster buttons. Each pulse removes two tenths. Under half a degree, ATT HOLD will engage and hold it there.
RCS QTYStop using the stick. If it reaches zero, full rewind on the tape transport spins the reels and takes roughly a degree per second out of the ship, which is how reaction wheels work on the real thing.

Warning

Silencing the alarm is not clearing the fault. The tone stops for thirty seconds and the lamp stays lit. If nothing has changed by the time it comes back, it comes back at the same level, and the twenty-second clock toward the next one is still running.

A word about the noise

The choir never stops. It ducks, thins and drones, but it keeps playing through every emergency, because it is the reason you came. Nothing on the deck flashes faster than twice a second. If your computer asks for reduced motion, the pulsing stops and the deck holds a still tint instead. And a first visit stays completely calm, because all of this takes deliberate input: press Power Up, listen, and you will never see an alarm at all.
