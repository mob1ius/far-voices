# Console compendium

The README's "toy" section gives each console item a one line history. This file
records where each line came from, how sure we are, and what was left out.

## Rules

1. A history line is written only if a source says it. No source, no line.
2. Items we made up say "Invented here" and carry no history.
3. Where sources disagree, the line is worded to survive both, and the dispute is
   noted below.
4. Searches were run on 17 November 2026. Where only secondary sources were
   found, that is stated.
5. Nothing here is a claim that the console is accurate. It is a toy. The lines
   describe the real things the props are modelled on.

## Entries

### Keypad (DSKY)
**Line used:** MIT's Instrumentation Laboratory was given Apollo's guidance in
August 1961, and the astronauts talked to its computer in two digit verbs and
nouns.
**Sources:** Eldon Hall's account at
[ibiblio.org/apollo](https://ibiblio.org/apollo/klabs/history/history_docs/designers_view.pdf)
and the [Apollo overview](https://ibiblio.org/apollo/ForDummies.html); the
[MIT Apollo guidance collection note](https://ed-thelen.org/comp-hist/vs-mit-apollo-guidance.html).
**Left out:** First flight year and clock speed. Mirrors disagree on the clock.

### Hand controller
**Line used:** Each Apollo command module couch had a rotational hand
controller, wired in parallel so the two backed each other up.
**Source:** NASA technical note D-7884,
[Apollo hand controllers](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/static/history/alsj/tnD7884HandContrlr.pdf).
**Left out:** Auction house descriptions of individual flown units, which are
dealer copy, not history.

### Caution lamps and master alarm
**Line used:** Apollo 11's descent set off four 1202 alarms and one 1201, and
Mission Control, having rehearsed a 1201 eleven days before launch, let the
landing continue.
**Sources:** NASA's
[Apollo 11 Lunar Surface Journal](https://nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/static/history/alsj/a11/a11.1201-pa.html)
page on the alarms; Discover, [Apollo 11's 1202 alarm explained](https://www.discovermagazine.com/apollo-11s-1202-alarm-explained-185).
**Left out:** An IFLScience piece claims a simulated 1202 caused an abort. It
contradicts the NASA page, so it was not used. The detail that the alarm list
sat under plexiglass comes from a secondary summary and was dropped.

### Attitude ball (FDAI)
**Line used:** Apollo's was nicknamed the 8-ball: pitch and yaw read off the
ball, roll off a pointer around its edge.
**Sources:** NASA's
[Apollo Lunar Surface Journal on the FDAI](https://www.nasa.gov/history/alsj/alsj-FDAI.html);
Ken Shirriff's teardown at [righto.com](https://www.righto.com/2025/06/).
**Left out:** Who designed it. One summary credits Lear Siegler and an auction
listing credits Honeywell for a command module unit. Not settled here.

### Station clocks and the network
**Line used:** NASA's Deep Space Network was formally founded in 1963, when
JPL's ground stations were tied into one network control centre.
**Source:** NASA,
[the Deep Space Network turns 60](https://www.nasa.gov/technology/space-comms/deep-space-network/nasas-deep-space-network-turns-60-and-prepares-for-the-future).
**Note:** The page gives the year and the anniversary of 24 December, and no
founder. We name none. A wiki mirror claims a January 1958 forerunner and was
not used.

### Event timer
**Line used:** Apollo carried an interval timer that worked like a kitchen timer
and rang a bell.
**Source:** Smithsonian National Air and Space Museum,
[Interval Timer, Apollo 11](https://www.si.edu/object/interval-timer-apollo-11:nasm_A19791534000).
**Left out:** The Apollo 13 knob fault. True, but not about this prop.

### Nixie counters
**Line used:** Burroughs introduced the Nixie tube in 1955, and who actually
invented it is still argued.
**Sources:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nixie_tube); Computer
History Museum,
[catalogue entry](https://www.computerhistory.org/collections/catalog/X1588.99A).
**Dispute:** The Computer History Museum says the design came from the Haydu
brothers around 1948 and was sold to Burroughs in 1952. Collector forums say
Haydu made no Nixie patent and that Burroughs's earliest is 1956. The line
states only the 1955 introduction and the dispute.

### Tape transport rotary knob (Leslie)
**Line used:** Don Leslie started on his rotating speaker in the late 1930s,
Hammond turned it down, and he built it himself. The rotary knob is a Leslie.
**Sources:** [Wikipedia, Leslie speaker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leslie_speaker);
NAMM Oral History, [Don Leslie](https://www.namm.org/library/oral-history/don-leslie).
**Note:** Sources give 1937 and 1940 for the prototype, hence "late 1930s". That
the knob is a Leslie is from the deck's own code, not a historical claim.

### Score recorder
**Line used:** Its chords come from Lyadov's Op. 61 of 1910 and Arkhangelsky's
Singing of Church Services of 1892.
**Source:** This repository's README, "Sources and licences", which cites the
Sibley Music Library scans on IMSLP. See also the music half of the README for
what was and was not extracted.

### Card reader
**Line used:** IBM's 80 column card appeared in 1928, a redesign of the cards
Hollerith built for the 1890 US census.
**Sources:** Douglas Jones, University of Iowa,
[Punched Cards](https://homepage.cs.uiowa.edu/~dwjones/cards/history.html);
Columbia University Computing History,
[keypunch](https://columbia.edu/cu/computinghistory/keypunch.html).
**Dispute:** Some sources say Britain's British Tabulating Machine Company used
rectangular holes first. A Computer History Museum volunteer newsletter argues
that is very unlikely. The line says IBM's card appeared in 1928, which holds
either way.

### Galley
**Line used:** Skylab's food tray was the first device to heat food in
spaceflight, by conduction. The microwave is ours.
**Source:** Smithsonian National Air and Space Museum,
[Tray, Food Heating, Skylab](https://www.airandspace.si.edu/es/collection-objects/tray-food-heating-skylab/nasm_A19810315000).
**Left out:** Anything about Gemini or Apollo food heating. Nothing found either
way.

### Globus
**Line used:** Soviet crews navigated with an electromechanical globe from
Vostok through Soyuz, until about 2002.
**Sources:** [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voskhod_Spacecraft_%22Globus%22_IMP_navigation_instrument);
Ken Shirriff, [Inside the Globus](https://www.righto.com/2023/01/inside-globus-ink-mechanical-navigation.html).
**Dispute:** One source says versions flew until the last Soyuz TM in April
2002, another says it was replaced starting with Soyuz TMA-1 in 2002. Hence
"about".

### Card file
**Line used:** The rotary card file was patented in January 1956 by Hildaur
Neilsen, an engineer at Arnold Neustadter's firm, and who deserves the credit is
still argued.
**Source:** [Wikipedia, Rolodex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rolodex), citing US
patent 2,731,966, granted 24 January 1956, assigned to Zephyr American.
**Dispute:** Neustadter is usually credited. Neilsen's son says his father
invented it. That is a family account and is treated as a dispute, not a verdict.
The Rolodex name's later sales history was left out.

### Slide projector
**Line used:** Kodak's Carousel went on sale in 1961 and the line ran until 2004.
**Sources:** Science Museum Group,
[Model 550R](https://collection.sciencemuseumgroup.org.uk/objects/co205500/kodak-carousel-projector-model-550r);
[Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carousel_slide_projector) for the
2004 end of the line and the 1965 tray patent.
**Left out:** One wiki mirror credits a Louis Misuraca with the original design.
Single source, not used.

### Golden Record
**Line used:** Voyager's record plays at 16⅔ rpm, half an LP, and its cover
carries diagrams on how to play it.
**Source:** NASA,
[Golden Record contents](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/voyager/golden-record-contents/):
"designed to be played at 16-2/3 revolutions per minute", with instructions "in
symbolic language" on the jacket.
**Left out:** The hydrogen time unit on the cover and Frank Drake's credit for
the markings. Both appear in secondary sources only
([Science Focus](https://www.sciencefocus.com/space/nasas-interstellar-mixtape-the-art-and-music-on-the-voyager-golden-record)).

### Teleprinter and tape punch
**Line used:** Donald Murray's 1901 system punched a tape first and sent from the
tape, the idea behind punches like this.
**Sources:** [Wikipedia, Teletype Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teletype_Corporation);
Columbia University Computing History,
[Teletype](https://columbia.edu/cu/computinghistory/teletype.html).
**Left out:** Any claim that NASA mission control used paper tape punches. The
only hit was an LLM generated encyclopedia entry, which is not a source.

### Remove Before Flight flags
**Line used:** Ground crews tie them to pins and covers that must come off before
flight. Where the custom began we could not establish.
**Sources:** None for origin. Aircraft supply catalogues describe current use,
and a 2021 Australian Transport Safety Bureau finding is that streamers alone are
an unreliable cue.
**Status:** Origin unverified. No history claimed.

### Peanut jar
**Line used:** JPL has passed peanuts around before critical events since Ranger
7 in 1964, which followed six failed Ranger missions.
**Sources:** NASA,
[What are JPL's lucky peanuts](https://science.nasa.gov/missions/what-are-jpls-lucky-peanuts);
JPL, [60 years of Ranger](https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/images/slice-of-history-60-years-of-ranger/).
**Dispute:** Accounts credit either Dick Wallace or mission manager Harris
Schurmeier with the first handout, so no one is named. NASA itself calls it a
superstition, not a cause of success.

### Pencil and card
**Line used:** NASA ordered mechanical pencils for Gemini in 1965. Fisher's pen
did not fly until Apollo 7 in 1968.
**Sources:** NASA History,
[The Space Pen](https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/eot2008/20090118045508/http://history.nasa.gov/spacepen.html)
(archived); NASA Spinoff, [space pens](https://spinoff.nasa.gov/space-pens).
**Note:** Patent year sources differ (1965 filing, 1966 grant), and NASA's order
date is variously 1967 or early 1968, so the line uses neither. The myth that
NASA paid to develop the pen is false: Fisher funded it, which is why it is not
mentioned here.

### Velcro
**Line used:** Patented in 1955 and adopted for Apollo, where Mike Collins
reported stray hooks floating into his eyes.
**Source:** NASA, [Apollo Lunar Surface Journal on Velcro](https://www.nasa.gov/history/alsj/WOTM/WOTM-Velcro.html).
**Left out:** The Velcro company's own figures on how much flew on Apollo 11,
which could not be checked against NASA documents. The 1955 patent date comes
from a secondary source in the same search.

### Porthole
**Line used:** A corned beef sandwich flew on Gemini 3 in March 1965, bought by
Wally Schirra and smuggled aboard by John Young, and a congressional committee
looked into it.
**Sources:** Smithsonian National Air and Space Museum,
[How an astronaut smuggled a sandwich into space](https://airandspace.si.edu/stories/editorial/how-astronaut-smuggled-sandwich-space);
NASA History, [fallout from the unauthorized Gemini III space sandwich](https://nasa.gov/history/fallout-from-the-unauthorized-gemini-iii-space-sandwich).

## Invented here, no history claimed

Advisory screen, abort switch, propellant and cabin thermometer readouts, the
main console's teleprinter and telemetry readout, annunciators and toggles, tape
library, cabin gauges, date drum, beverage stores, ARM switches, percolator and
mug, the microwave, and every drifting object outside the lower porthole.

## Not looked into

These are on the console and have no line in the README because we have not
researched them: the reel to reel transport, the percolator, the camera in the
net, and the printed warning plates. If one gets a line later, it gets an entry here
first.

## Method note

Searches were run through a general web search and the most useful pages were
fetched where a claim carried weight (the Deep Space Network founding and the
Golden Record speed). Several entries rest on Wikipedia and museum catalogue
pages. Where a claim could be checked against a NASA or museum page that was done,
and where it could not, the entry says so.
